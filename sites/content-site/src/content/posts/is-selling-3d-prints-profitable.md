Short answer: **per item, yes, easily. Per hour of your life, it depends entirely on volume and how much handling each order needs.** That distinction is the whole game, and it's why two shops with identical products end up with completely different outcomes.

Let's do a real month.

## A realistic month at 30 orders

Assume a small shop selling on Etsy: 30 orders, average item price $22, average print 45 g and 6 hours, 20 minutes hands-on per order.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue (30 × $22) | $660.00 |
| Etsy fees (about 13% effective, including fixed fees) | −$86.00 |
| Filament | −$30.00 |
| Electricity + machine wear | −$32.00 |
| Packaging | −$22.50 |
| Failure allowance (8%) | −$5.00 |
| **Left before your labor** | **$484.50** |

Now the part most breakdowns skip. Thirty orders × 20 minutes = **10 hours** of fulfillment. Add roughly 5 hours of listing, photography, messages, and admin. Call it **15 hours**.

```
$484.50 ÷ 15 hours = about $32/hour
```

That's a genuinely decent number — better than most side work. So why do so many print shops quietly fold?

## The three things that break it

**1. Getting to 30 orders.** This is the real constraint, and it isn't a printing problem. Most new print shops don't reach 30 orders a month in their first year. Everything above is arithmetic; demand is the hard part, and it comes from product choice and listing quality, not from your printer.

**2. Design and setup time isn't in that table.** The evenings spent modeling, test-printing, photographing, and writing listings are real hours. Amortized over a product that sells 200 units they vanish; over one that sells 4, they're the entire cost of the project. Plenty of designs sell 4.

**3. Small orders don't scale the way you hope.** Doubling to 60 orders roughly doubles revenue *and* roughly doubles hands-on time — unless you batch. A shop that grows without changing its process just buys itself a lower-paying job with more boxes in the hallway.

## What the profitable shops do differently

The ones that work aren't printing faster or buying cheaper filament. They're doing some combination of:

- **Batching aggressively.** One plate, eight units, one setup. This attacks the only expensive input — your hands.
- **Selling higher-ticket items.** A $60 item and a $12 item can take similar handling time. Fixed per-order costs of roughly $1.20 (fees plus packaging) are 2% of one and 10% of the other.
- **Picking prints that need almost no post-processing.** Support removal is unpaid labor.
- **Chasing repeat and volume orders.** Wholesale, local businesses, event orders — 40 units to one buyer is one setup and one shipment.
- **Selling the design, not only the print.** Digital files carry no material, postage, or handling cost. Different business, much better margin shape, much more competition.

## The honest summary

3D printing is a **low-margin-per-hour, decent-margin-per-item** business where the binding constraint is demand and handling time — not filament, not electricity, and not printer speed.

If you price properly (labor, machine wear, failures, and fees all included) and you can generate steady orders, roughly $25–$35 an hour is realistic. If you price on "filament × 3", you'll be busy, look profitable on paper, and be paying for the privilege of running your printer.

The only way to know which one you're doing is to measure it.
