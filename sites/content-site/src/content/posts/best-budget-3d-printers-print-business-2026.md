When you're printing to *sell*, the sticker price of a printer is almost the least important number. What matters is **cost per reliable print hour**: a cheap machine that jams every third print and needs constant babysitting can be far more expensive than a slightly pricier one that just runs. Remember, your printer's cost is a real per-hour line in your [pricing math](../how-to-price-3d-prints/) — a machine that lasts longer literally lowers the price you need to charge.

Here's how to think about budget printers for a small print business in 2026. We're deliberately talking in **categories** rather than pretending to have bench-tested every unit, because that's the honest version.

## What actually matters for a seller

| Factor | Why it matters to your margin |
|---|---|
| **Reliability / failure rate** | Every failed print is wasted filament, power, and *your* labor. This dwarfs the purchase price over time. |
| **Speed** | Faster prints = more sellable units per day from the same machine and the same you. |
| **Running costs** | Standard 1.75 mm filament and common nozzles keep material and spare-part costs low. Proprietary consumables quietly tax every print. |
| **Ease of use** | Auto-leveling and good first-layer behavior cut the labor and reprints that eat small-item profit. |
| **Resale / longevity** | A popular, well-supported model holds value and has cheap spares, extending its usable life (and lowering depreciation per print). |

## The categories that make sense on a budget

**1. Mainstream "bedslinger" FDM printers (~entry price).**
The sweet spot for most people starting out. Modern budget FDM machines with auto-leveling and 1.75 mm filament are cheap to run and easy to find parts for. Great for functional parts, toys, organizers, and most Etsy-style products. Look for models with a large owner community — that community is your free troubleshooting support.

**2. CoreXY / high-speed FDM (a step up).**
If demand grows, faster CoreXY machines dramatically increase units-per-day. Higher upfront cost, but if you're selling volume, the extra throughput can pay for itself. Only worth it once you have consistent orders — don't buy speed you can't fill.

**3. Resin (MSLA) — a specialist tool, not a starter.**
Unbeatable detail for miniatures and jewelry, but resin adds cost and labor: consumables, IPA washing, UV curing, ventilation, gloves, and disposal. The per-part *labor* is higher, which changes your pricing. Add resin only when your product genuinely needs the detail.

## How to choose in practice

1. **Match the printer to your product.** Functional/large → budget FDM. Miniatures/high-detail → resin, eyes open to the extra labor. High volume → faster CoreXY.
2. **Prioritize reliability over sticker price.** A $50 saving that costs you five failed prints and an hour of fixing per week is a bad trade.
3. **Check consumable costs before buying.** Standard 1.75 mm filament and common nozzles = cheap to run. Proprietary anything = ongoing tax.
4. **Buy the community, not just the machine.** A model with lots of owners means cheap spares, free profiles, and fast answers.
